About __SITE_NAME__

Free 3D sculptures for learning to draw light and form.

Drawing a figure convincingly comes down to one thing: seeing how light turns across a form. __SITE_NAME__ gives you sculptures to study that with. Pick one, turn it to any angle, put the lamp wherever you like, and draw what you see.

Why sculpture

For centuries, art academies taught drawing in cast rooms, halls of plaster copies of famous sculpture. Before students drew a live model, they drew casts. A cast holds still for as long as you need, and its single pale color hides nothing: no skin tones, no patterns, no glare, just form and the light on it. You can see clearly where the light side ends, where the form shadow begins, how cast shadows fall, and how reflected light lifts the dark side.

Here the casts are 3D scans of real sculptures, and the light is yours to move. One sculpture can give you a hundred different studies.

Ways to practice

  • Start with the Asaro head. Its flat planes make it easy to see where the light turns. Then look for the same planes in a real face, like Napoléon or the Pseudo-Seneca.
  • One sculpture, three lights. Draw the same view lit from the front, from the side, and from behind, and see how much the form seems to change.
  • Two values only. Squint and split everything into light and shadow before you add any halftones. The plain marble makes the shapes easy to read.
  • Quick studies. Turn the model a little every few minutes and draw it again. Short poses train your eye faster than one long one.

Using the viewer

  • Drag to turn the model. Scroll or pinch to zoom, and right-drag to pan.
  • Drag the bright spot on the ball in the top right to move the light, or use the arrow keys. Drag it into the dotted outer ring to put the light behind the model.
  • Double-click any part, like a face or a hand, to turn and zoom around it. The crosshair button does the same with a single click, and the arrows button brings the whole model back.
  • The bookmark button saves your exact view, light included, and the button below it takes you back to it. Each model remembers its saved view in your browser.
  • The slider under the ball sets the background from black to light gray. It stays set on every page.

The light stays fixed to the model as you turn it, just like a lamp in a studio.

The Asaro head

The Asaro head is a human head reduced to flat, angled planes. On a plane head each surface faces one way, so it takes a single value. That shows you plainly where the light turns, where the form shadow starts, and where shadows fall under the brow, nose and lip. Once you know the planes, you start to see them in every face.

John Asaro, an American painter born in 1937, developed the Planes of the Head while teaching figurative painting at Art Center College of Design in Pasadena. The physical head has been available since 1976. The one here is “Asaro Head” by AgentSCAD, a fan recreation shared under CC BY-SA. It is not the official sculpture, which you can buy from planesofthehead.com.

Where the models come from

The sculptures are scans made in museums, from three sources: Three D Scans, an archive started by Oliver Laric that publishes its scans free of copyright restrictions; Scan the World on MyMiniFactory; and SMK, the National Gallery of Denmark, which has released its plaster casts into the public domain. Each scan was converted for the web and given one plain marble finish. Scans shared under CC BY-NC-SA keep that license here.
